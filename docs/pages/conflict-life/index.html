A camp is no longer a rule. It is three people, a chief, a war leader and an elder, each with a temper of his own. It gets hungry in winter, angry when you refuse its envoy, proud, restless, afraid. It guesses how strong you are and is sometimes badly wrong, remembers who killed its chief, and decides with one of those guesses in hand. The fight itself stays the engine's, and certain, so every lost raid is a council that guessed wrong, and every fight has a reason it can tell you. How a camp decides, in plain words

Everything below is a number the engine already has or can keep cheaply between events. Each culture has its own values in its data file, so goblins, bandits, raiders, the Umvuthana and the Frostmaw think alike in shape and differ in temper.

A council of three

Each camp has a chief, a war leader who is also his heir, and an elder. Each has a temperament: how bold, how aggressive, how sociable. When the camp sits, one of them has the floor, drawn by standing; his temper carries the day and the other two soften it. So the same camp, in the same state, is bolder on the days the war leader speaks.

The goblin culture's average council. Each camp draws its own three around these, with a spread of 0.2, so two goblin camps in one world behave differently, and a camp changes when its chief does.

  • Standing moves. A won fight lifts the standing of whoever argued for it. A lost one cuts it and hands the floor to the elder. A hothead who wins keeps winning the argument until he loses.
  • Chiefs come and go. They die of a fever, in a quarrel or a hunting accident, are deposed, grow old, or fall leading a beaten attack. The war leader takes over with his own temper and a season to prove himself, a year if his chief was killed. He keeps part of each grudge, more if he is aggressive.

What it wants, and what it fears

  • FoodIts stores fill in summer and run down in winter, more or less in a good or a bad year. A hungry camp values loot; a fed one hardly at all.
  • LootWhat a raid would carry home, planned against what the camp last saw you holding, not what you really hold.
  • RevengeThe grievance today's engine already keeps, from your mines and smoke, a culture's old hatreds and the railway.
  • HonourWounded pride after a refused envoy, a killed envoy, a raid, a stolen crystal, a dead chief. It fades, and it counts for little when the camp has no hope of winning.
  • GloryWarriors grow restless the longer they go without a fight. A new war leader must prove himself.
  • FearOf losses: its own dead, a lost fight, a dragon strike. A frightened camp counts every risk higher. Raiders also fear a neighbour grown too strong, and may strike first.

What it believes, and what it remembers

  • A guess at each neighbour's strength, with an uncertainty. For camps and villages it has always known, the guess starts within about a third. For a new player base it starts wide: about 15, where anything from 5 to 40 seems plausible.
  • Looking sharpens it. A fight is the best look, then scouts, then an envoy who walked to your gate and back, then rumour. Left alone the guess blurs again over a year or two, four times slower for a player's base, because walls do not fall down.
  • A defeat teaches. A base that destroyed a party is at least that strong, and the camp remembers that for a while.
  • Word that you are away halves its guess of you for 3 to 10 days: your walls are home, you are not. It changes nothing it believes about your walls.
  • A record of who pays. How often you have paid its envoys, fading back toward even over two years.
  • Up to four memories about each neighbour, the strongest kept, each fading: "player Bea killed Vorr, our chief, in the spring of year 3", "we lost 26 at player Ben", "player Ben refused our envoy", "Crowsnest's hunters killed 2 of ours at the border", "word came that the trains ride without guards", and the kind ones, "player Bea paid 87". A grievance says how angry the camp is; a memory says why.

How it chooses

  1. One voice takes the floor, drawn by standing.
  2. For each neighbour before it, the council draws one guess of that neighbour's strength from its belief. A wide belief sometimes gives a guess low enough to try: that is how a camp tests a new base, and how it is sometimes badly wrong. A bold speaker guesses his enemies weaker.
  3. Against that guess it weighs each thing it could do: wait, warn (a howl, a roar, the gong, a war band shown at your edge), send scouts, send an envoy for tribute, send a band, a raid or an invasion, fall on a rival camp's hunters, lie in wait for a train or a caravan, offer a truce, or, for the Umvuthana, send the raptors after a village's animals.
  4. Each act scores what it might win, less the warriors it might lose, plus revenge, honour and glory, less the cost of breaking a sworn truce or making a new enemy.
  5. It then picks among staying home and its best idea for each neighbour: usually the best, sometimes the second best, rarely something rash. An aggressive council is more erratic.

Terms first, when terms make sense

An envoy asks only where the camp's threat is credible. The price falls between what the war is worth to the camp and the loot it believes it would carry off, nearer the top when the speaker is aggressive or hungry. Both sides are better off with the payment than with the war. The envoy walks to your gate and back, so the camp learns your walls and your stock whether or not you pay. A payment buys a truce of about 48 days, sworn by the chief who took it. Breaking it costs him three times what it costs his heir, which is how heirs come to raid a payer. A refusal wounds the camp's pride and is remembered. A camp that has been paid for years does not lightly kill the goose.

What keeps a world stable over decades
  • A camp attacked meets its guard, a quarter of its people (a grove stands all its warriors and the Umvuthi around the throne). Without that, no full camp was ever beaten and camps never fought each other.
  • A lost fight destroys the party, frightens the camp, hands the floor to the elder and teaches it a floor. A raid that wins on a camp costs the defenders, which feeds their grievance.
  • A won fight sates vengeance: it halves the grievance and takes most of the wound away.
  • Survivors walk home for 1 to 3 days, and the camp is weaker until they are back.
  • A fed camp values loot little: most fights come in winter and after bad years.
  • A camp afraid or sociable enough offers a truce, and both chiefs swear it. In 64 worlds of 50 years, 1,562 truces were sworn; the chiefs who swore them broke them 55 times, later chiefs 261 times.
  • Making a new enemy, or fighting one's own culture, is dear.
  • Hunters of two hostile camps meet at the border about once in two years when both are fed, two or three times a year when both starve. Each clash is remembered, and some become feuds. Without them, camps at strength drifted into permanent peace.

The fight stays the engine's, and certain

A lost raid is a council that guessed wrong

When a party meets a defence, the engine already decides it with no dice: the stronger side wins, and how many each side loses follows from the ratio. This design keeps that exactly. The doubt is in the council's head, not in the fight. It does not know how strong you are; it guesses. So every lost raid has a story: a new base guessed weak, a guard guessed thin, a player believed away.

The gate you rejected rolled odds against a fight that has no dice. It treated its belief as a chance to win, so it thought a weaker party had a fair chance, and sent it. That is why it attacked camps it could not beat (75% of its fights on camps were lost, 99% of those on the fortress) and left alone targets it would have beaten.

The one fight that is drawn is one you are at in person outside the raid director: waking the Frostmaw while you steal its crystal is Minecraft's own fight.

Before the first fight

A goblin camp's guess of your new base: wide, around 15. Every guess in the shaded part puts your walls below its invasion of 30.4, so attacking looks worth it. Your walls are 80.

After the lost invasion

All 26 died. The camp now knows roughly how strong you are, and nothing below its own invasion is believed any more. Years later the guess blurs again, slowly; word that you are away halves it for a few days. That is when the rare second test comes.

Today a refused envoy is followed by a raid 9 or 10 days later, every time, and under the gate 3 days later. In this design you cannot call it, and at a fortress most refusals are never answered with a fight at all.

Each side plays close to its best reply

For a weak base, refusing costs everything, paying costs 43% of its income, building a little and paying costs 35%, and walls of 80 cost nothing a year. So your best reply is to pay or to build, and the camp's best reply to a payer is to ask, not raid: 0.1 fights a year on him against 3.4 on the refuser. Toward a fortress its best reply is to leave it alone, and it nearly does. Each side plays near its best reply with some noise, and the noise is what makes the world feel alive. Under the ladder the camp plays its worst reply against strength: 9.6 lost wars a year at a fortress in the one-on-one runs, 65 dead.

  3. Y1 d83The Grey Outpost has asked her for nothing. Its band of 8 marches on her anyway, wins, loses 3 and carries off 43. Its reason: "Loot."

  5. Y2 to Y5Both camps' envoys keep coming, about three a year between them, asking 68 to 127. She pays every one. The Grey Outpost's envoys say "again: you paid 100 in the winter of year 2"; they have learned she pays.
  6. Y5 d62Ashpit's chief, a later Nub, takes 93 from her and swears a truce.

Alliances: two camps against a third

You answered CL-5 with "Add alliances". CONFLICT-LIFE.md section 2.16 now has them, and the model runs them on the same 64 worlds of 50 years, each paired with the same seed run with alliances off. In short: two camps that share a foe may swear against it, call each other to fight, fight as one party, and fall out. They are rare, and a player almost never meets one.

How a pact works

  • Who can swearTwo camps that reach each other and both reach a foe: a camp, a village or a player, never the railway or a caravan. Both quarrel with it (a standing hate, a grievance of 30 or more, or wounded pride), or one does and the other is kin. The Frostmaw never swears; bandits swear most readily. At most 4 pacts a camp, and while one runs, the two camps' hunters do not clash at the border.
  • Why a camp swearsFor what a joint war would win that it could not win alone, and for the guard an ally gives. Kin count for it, a hated culture strongly against, and sending to swear has a cost. The camp asked weighs it the same way, refuses outright if it holds a grudge or wounded pride against the asker, and remembers a refusal for 48 days.
  • The oathBoth chiefs swear. A pact runs about two game years on average, drawn, and the two camps share what each believes about the foe. While it runs, a sworn camp calls its ally instead of marching alone with a party the ally could join.
  • The callThe ally may be busy (no blame), come, or refuse. A refusal shames the oath, three times over for the chief who swore it, strains the pact and wounds the caller's pride: "Mudhollow would not come against the Grey Outpost in the winter of year 3." The caller may then go alone.
  • The joint fightStill the engine's, and certain. At a camp or a village it is one fight on the sum, each contingent losing the same share and splitting the take by what it can carry; at a base, one threat a class. Each camp books its own dead and its own memory. The target blames both, and its own allies are provoked.
  • After the fightA win warms the pact; a defeat strains it, more for the ally than the caller. An ally that sent less than four fifths of what was counted on is resented for it. Strain fades by half in 96 days.
  • How pacts endMost lapse (four in five). Others break under strain, after refusals, when a camp is sworn to both sides of a fight, or in betrayal.
  • BetrayalA sworn camp that falls on its ally pays the oath and more. The betrayed takes it as the gravest insult, with a lasting memory, "Ashpit broke its oath and fell on us in the summer of year 12", and every camp that knows the betrayer trusts its word less. The oath binds the chief who held the seat at the swearing; his heir is bound only once: "Gitz was not chief when Mudhollow swore."
  • DeterrenceBefore it attacks, a camp counts the target's sworn allies that can reach it. Camps know each other's pacts; a player hears of a pact only as news a traveller carries (below).
  • Joint demandsAn envoy may name the ally as the threat: "Mudhollow would come with us." The price is set on the joint take, the payment is split, and the ally is bound by the truce.
  • What you hearThe envoy names the ally: "You killed Vorr, our chief, in the spring of year 3. Ashpit and Mudhollow ask 140 emeralds." Nothing about a pact comes to you in chat (CL-9). That two camps have sworn, against you or against another, reaches you as news from the next trader: "Ashpit and Mudhollow have sworn against you." A party's reason names both camps: "beside the Grey Outpost, sworn against player Ben"; it is the story's and the news's, not a line shown when the party arrives.
  • What you seeTwo columns of two cultures from two camps, each marching its own route and arriving on one tick. They share one spawn quota of 16 bodies, split by bodies (a raptor pack counts 5), so two invasions show about 8 of each at first. Allies do not turn on each other: the bridge cancels a target change toward a body of the same joint attack, with no mixin and no scoreboard team. A stray blow still hurts.
  • Peaceful, uninstall, the groveOn Peaceful pacts may be sworn, but no call is made and no joint party leaves. Nothing new is added to the world and no record id is new: the ally rides on the envoy and march records, so UR-1 is unchanged. The Sun Grove may swear and its raid can be called; its invasion still goes only on the Umvuthi's own pride, aloft at the rear.

What MZ-15 already settles

Your MZ-15, that whoever a grove sends marches visibly, wins wherever it and this design differ. The design takes it as it is.

  • Every party marches. A band walks its route from its camp to the target and back: real bodies where a player is, the engine beyond, its position known at every tick so the far view can draw it from about a kilometre away. This design adds no march record of its own. Every act the council picks is one of today's party kinds and rides the same march: a skirmish's route ends at the rival camp's edge, a caravan ambush's on the road, a lurk's at its ambush spot by the track.
  • The Umvuthi goes with its band, aloft at the rear of the column (your MZ-16, "Aloft"). Its strength is in the band's (12.6 for a full invasion), and missing from the grove's defence until it is home. It leads only when its pride is wounded, and if the band is destroyed it falls with it, and the grove waits for an heir.
  • Item 11: the census never ends a march. When a camp comes into range while its party is out, the party's bodies are taken and it marches on. A living member away is counted with its party, not called home. A party ends only at its own home or at its last death. The cost is UR-1's: members away when rtsim is uninstalled are lost.
  • The 128-block rule holds. A war party on the march is not an arrival: its members are its camp's own, already walking toward you, so the rule does not apply to them. Its bodies appear where its march puts them as they come inside the ticking area, a stack at a time (a Sun Grove column comes as rows of warriors, a raptor pack, the chief alone). None of them is drawn where it appears; they walk into sight, and nothing appears that was not already walking toward you. A party that reaches you with no bodies yet (you log in or teleport there as it arrives) walks in along its own route from the farthest ticking point. The rule's other half stands: an arrival (a recruit, a new Frostmaw, a new Umvuthi) is never placed within 128 blocks of a player. That edge is 160 to 176 blocks off along an axis at a simulation distance of 10, vanilla's default, and 128 to 144 at 8. The Umvuthi, the one body drawn beyond 128 blocks, comes in aloft at the rear: at 8 it can be seen to appear 128 to 132 blocks off, never nearer.
  • The spawn quota does not shrink a band. At most 16 members are in Minecraft at once. A goblin invasion of 26 keeps its whole strength in the fight; the rest walk in along the route as the quota frees, so you see it arrive in two or three bunches. The two contingents of a joint party share one quota of 16.

News in the world: no chat notices for conflict

You answered CL-9 with "Shouldn't be in chat notifs, but they should be able to learn about this stuff by travelling traders, etc.", and then made it the rule for all of it: "All faction news in-world". CONFLICT-LIFE.md section 2.17 is the design.

  • What staysThe cues: the howl, the roar, the gong and a war band shown at the edge of your land, which are sounds and sights and carry no text. And the envoy, who says why in person (CL-1).
  • What is newsA feud opening or climbing; a pact sworn, broken or betrayed; a raid, an invasion or a skirmish; a chief or a champion falling. Each is a report with the day, the place and the two sides, and its words are written from the camp's reasons when it is read.
  • How it travelsAs all rtsim news does. Those who saw it learn it at once: the camp itself, and the village or base it fell on. Everyone else learns it only from a traveller: messengers walk each road four times a day, and caravans and bands carry gossip on their circuits. News spreads at the speed of trade, and a village nobody trades with learns nothing.
  • How long it lastsA feud 10 days, a pact or a betrayal 30, a raid 20, a death 30. These are placeholders.
  • What it does not doNews changes no one's feelings and never enters vanilla villager gossip: a rumour of a feud between two camps is not a villager's grudge. Your own deeds still move a village, as today.
  • What goesThe text of every conflict notice, the told pact, the pact notices CL-9 (a) offered, and every line of chat. A player who lives near both camps and trades with no one hears nothing, which is the point.
  • NowAn agent is removing the chat notices the game sends today and routing them to news. How you hear the news is the one open question, CL-10.

Building it

The council is not built yet. Two of its pieces are being built inside the Mowzie build: the march record and the walk-in from the edge. The order, by what gets built:

  1. The council on the engine's test harness, with no Java: feuds toward camps and villages, the sittings, drives, beliefs, the choice, the price, truces, succession, defender losses and the reason codes, driving today's party kinds.
  2. Saving the new fields, and the paged sweep that measures them at scale.
  3. The reasons in the game: an envoy's spoken line, and the news a feud, a pact, a raid or a death makes, with their GameTests.
  4. Train ambushes for every culture that ambushes, once the Mowzie plan's lurk and track sampler exist.
  5. A stolen crystal as a theft, once the Mowzie plan reports who holds it.
  6. The walk-in, once MZ-15's march record exists.
  7. Caravan ambushes: the world answers which caravans pass a camp, and the robbery's loss reaches the economy.
  8. Alliances (section 2.16): pacts, calls, the joint fight and march, strain and betrayal on the engine's harness first; then the ally's name on the envoy and in the news, allies that do not turn on each other, and the shared quota, with their GameTests.

What it costs

  • Memory: about 45 bytes a faction and 96 a feud, and for pacts 16 a faction and about 40 a camp feud. A faction's saved row grows from about 450 bytes to 1.6 to 1.9 KB, but rows are paged to disk, so resident memory grows only by about 0.4 MB at 4,096 factions.
  • Step CPU: a sitting replaces today's plunder clock about one for one, and its arithmetic is small beside loading the faction's page. Estimated 52 to 60 microseconds a faction-day against today's 52.3 at 1,024 factions.
  • Save CPU: estimated about three times today's, about 120 to 140 ms a game day at 1,024 factions and about 900 at 4,096. The paged sweep measures it; if it is too much, the new feuds move to a page of their own.
  • Nothing scans per tick, and nothing grows with time: memories are four slots, beliefs two numbers.
  • Size: about 4,600 lines (estimate), about 1,750 of them tests; alliances are about 1,380 of the total.

CPU figures are inferred from today's measurements, not measured.

What the model leaves out
  • The march out: in the model a fight resolves the day it is decided; the engine's marches add days before it. The walk home is modelled.
  • The watched fight: every fight is the abstract one except the Frostmaw woken at its den. The raid director's waves, the spawn quota and the walk-in are not modelled.
  • Players are scripts: a fortress, a payer, a hunter, a thief, a railway owner. Real players change.
  • One valley of seven factions, two villages, a road and one line.
  • Dragon strikes and lost hunting grounds are the model's stand-ins for the world, not part of the design.
